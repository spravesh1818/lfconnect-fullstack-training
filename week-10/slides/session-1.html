Part 1

The Sprint

A sprint is a short, time-boxed period (typically 2 weeks) during which the team builds a potentially shippable product increment.

Sprint anatomy

  • Duration: 1–4 weeks. Most teams use 2-week sprints. Never extended mid-sprint.
  • Sprint Goal: a single, clear objective for the sprint. Every backlog item selected serves this goal.
  • Time-boxing: the sprint ends on schedule even if all items are not done. Unfinished work goes back to the backlog.
  • No-scope-change rule: do not add work mid-sprint unless something is removed. Protect the team's focus.
  • Cadence: same days each sprint. Predictability builds trust and planning muscle.

Backlog refinement

Ongoing work, not a ceremony — the PO and team groom backlog items before they enter a sprint.

  • Break large items into sprint-sized stories
  • Write acceptance criteria so "done" is unambiguous
  • Estimate complexity (story points or T-shirt sizes)
  • Remove items that are no longer relevant
  • Target: top 2 sprints of backlog always "ready" (estimated and acceptance-criteria written)

Part 2

Sprint Planning

The team selects backlog items they can complete and commits to a sprint goal. This is a planning meeting, not a status update.

Running Sprint Planning

  1. PO presents the sprint goal — why this sprint matters and what success looks like.
  2. Team reviews capacity — who is on holiday? Any interviews? Realistic developer-days available.
  3. Select stories — pull from the top of the product backlog until capacity is full.
  4. Break stories into tasks — each story decomposed into implementation sub-tasks (usually <1 day each).
  5. Confirm sprint goal — the team agrees they can achieve it with what was selected.

Capacity planning

  • Velocity — the team's average story points completed per sprint (from the last 3 sprints).
  • Never plan to 100% capacity — unplanned bugs, meetings, and reviews consume 20–30 % of time.
  • Focus factor: velocity ÷ available dev-days. Typical range: 0.6–0.7.
  • New team? Start with a conservative commitment and calibrate after 3–4 sprints.

Part 3

Daily Standup

A 15-minute daily sync for the development team to inspect progress toward the sprint goal and adapt the day's plan.

Three questions (classic format)

  • What did I complete since the last standup?
  • What will I work on today?
  • Do I have any blockers?

Keep answers short. Blockers are surfaced here, not solved here — take the discussion offline after the standup ends.

Standup anti-patterns

  • Status report to the manager — it's for the team, not management. SM facilitates; managers listen only.
  • Problem-solving during standup — "let's debug that now" kills the time-box. Park it.
  • Skipping blockers — if you're blocked, say so. Unblocking teammates is the SM's job.
  • Not updating the board — the board should reflect reality before the standup starts.
  • Too long — if it's consistently 30+ minutes, restructure. Split large teams.

Part 4

Sprint Review and Retrospective

Sprint Review

  • Goal: inspect the increment and adapt the product backlog based on feedback.
  • Who attends: the entire Scrum team plus stakeholders (PM, design, sales, customers).
  • Format: demo working software (not slides). Walk through the sprint goal and what was completed.
  • Outcome: updated product backlog reflecting new priorities based on feedback.
  • Time-box: 4 hours for a 4-week sprint; 2 hours for 2-week sprints.

Sprint Retrospective

Internal team ceremony — no stakeholders. Inspect the process, not the product.

  • What went well? (keep doing these things)
  • What could be improved? (experiment next sprint)
  • Action items: 1–3 specific, owned improvements. Not a wish list — commitments.

Popular format: Start / Stop / Continue — what should we start, stop, and keep doing?

Part 5

Team Metrics

Velocity and burndown

  • Velocity — story points completed per sprint. Stabilises after 3–4 sprints. Use for forecasting, not pressure.
  • Burndown chart — daily: total remaining story points vs. ideal straight line to zero. A burndown that flattens means blocked work or scope creep.
  • Burnup chart — shows total scope and completed work. Better for visualising scope changes.
  • Use metrics to improve, not as a management tool to compare individuals. Story points measure complexity, not output or speed.

Definition of Done vs Acceptance Criteria

Definition of Done

  • Applies to every item
  • Set by the team
  • Examples: tests pass, code reviewed, deployed to staging, no critical linting errors
  • The quality bar — same every sprint

Acceptance Criteria

  • Specific to one story
  • Set by the Product Owner
  • Examples: user can log in with email; error shows if email is invalid
  • The feature spec — different every story
